EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Urban road infrastructure is critical to the functioning of modern smart cities, yet road
surfaces are subject to continuous degradation caused by vehicular loading, weather-
ing, and deferred maintenance. Road hazards such as potholes, surface cracks, and
open or displaced manhole covers represent significant threats to public safety and in-
cur substantial economic costs for municipal authorities worldwide. The conventional
approach to road inspection — manual field surveys — is labour-intensive, geograph-
ically incomplete, and inherently subjective. Advances in computer vision and deep
learning have created an opportunity to automate the detection and classification of
road hazards at scale.
This thesis presents the design, implementation, and evaluation of a novelQuantum-
Enhanced Deep Learning Framework for Road Hazard Safety Analysis. The pro-
posed system introduces a two-stage, modular pipeline that deliberately decouples the
tasks of hazard localisation and hazard classification. In the detection stage, a YOLO-
based object detector is employed to identify and localise regions of interest within
full-resolution road images. In the classification stage, the extracted hazard crops are
processed by a hybrid quantum-classical classifier that integrates a pre-trained ResNet-
18 convolutional backbone, a dimensionality-reducing multilayer perceptron (MLP),
and a 4-qubit Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC).
The ResNet-18 backbone, pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, extracts a rich 512-
dimensional feature vector from each 64×64 cropped input image using residual learn-
ing. A two-layer MLP then compresses this high-dimensional representation to a com-
pact 4-dimensional latent vector, matching the dimensionality required for quantum
encoding. The VQC encodes each latent dimension onto a dedicated qubit via parame-
terisedR Y rotation gates (angle encoding), applies six variational layers with trainable
single-qubit rotations and ring-topology CNOT entanglement gates, and produces out-
put measurements as Pauli-Zexpectation values. A classical linear classification head
maps these quantum measurements to class logits, and a softmax function yields the
final class probability distribution over four hazard categories: cracks, potholes, open
manholes, and garbage.
The system is trained on a custom class-balanced dataset constructed by merging
iii
two publicly available annotated road hazard repositories, totalling 6,797 images across
four hazard classes. To address class imbalance, random downsampling of the majority
class is applied, resulting in a final training set of 3,340 images with approximately
uniform class representation. All quantum gradients are computed using the parameter-
shift rule, enabling seamless end-to-end backpropagation through the hybrid model.
Experimental evaluation demonstrates that the proposed hybrid model (Classical
Dataset + Quantum Model configuration) achieves a macro-averaged F1-score of 0.8439,
a precision of 0.8579, and a recall of 0.8451, which is competitive with the purely clas-
sical baseline (F1 = 0.8016). Per-class analysis shows strong performance across all
hazard categories, with potholes achieving an F1-score of 0.92, cracks 0.82, garbage
0.88, and open manholes 0.79. Training convergence analysis confirms that the integra-
tion of the VQC does not introduce optimisation instability.
This work constitutes a rigorous proof-of-concept for quantum-augmented infras-
tructure monitoring and contributes to the emerging field of quantum machine learn-
ing applied to real-world safety applications. Future directions include deployment on
physical NISQ quantum processors, noise-robust variational ansatz design, and integra-
tion with real-time urban monitoring platforms.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND
Urban road networks form a fundamental part of modern infrastructure, supporting the
movement of people, goods, and services in everyday life. As cities continue to grow
and traffic density increases, maintaining the structural condition of road surfaces be-
comes increasingly important, not only for transportation efficiency but also for public
safety. However, road surfaces naturally degrade over time due to factors such as re-
peated vehicular loading, temperature variations, moisture, and delayed maintenance.
This deterioration typically appears in the form of potholes, surface cracks, and exposed
or displaced manhole covers.
These road hazards are a serious issue in both developed and developing regions.
Potholes, for instance, can cause tyre damage, suspension issues, and misalignment,
leading to significant repair costs. Cracks, if not addressed early, tend to expand and
develop into larger structural failures, increasing maintenance costs over time. Open
or displaced manholes pose immediate risks to vehicles and pedestrians. In addition to
physical damage, such hazards can contribute to accidents and also increase the finan-
cial burden on municipal authorities.
Traditionally, road inspection is carried out through manual surveys conducted by
trained personnel. These inspections may be done on foot or using vehicles equipped
with specialised instruments. While this approach can provide accurate assessments, it
is difficult to scale across large road networks. Cities often have thousands of kilometres
of roads, making frequent and complete inspections impractical. Moreover, manual
inspections can vary depending on the individual performing them, and lower-priority
roads may not be inspected regularly.
With the increasing availability of high-resolution cameras, UA Vs, and vehicle-
mounted sensing systems, there is now a large amount of visual data available for
automated analysis. These systems continuously capture road surface images under
different conditions. However, converting this raw data into useful insights requires
automated systems that can reliably detect and classify different types of road hazards
1
under varying lighting, weather, and environmental conditions.
1.2 MOTIV ATION
Although deep learning has shown strong performance in road hazard detection, several
challenges still remain:
1.Intra-class Variability:Hazards within the same category can look very dif-
ferent. For example, potholes vary in size and shape, and cracks can appear in
multiple forms such as longitudinal, transverse, or irregular patterns. This varia-
tion makes classification difficult, especially with limited training data.
2.Inter-class Similarity:Some hazard types share similar visual features. For
instance, shadows may resemble cracks, and debris may partially hide potholes.
This makes it harder for models to clearly distinguish between classes.
3.Data Scarcity and Imbalance:Compared to large-scale datasets, road hazard
datasets are relatively small. Additionally, some classes appear more frequently
than others, leading to imbalance and biased model predictions.
4.Generalisation Across Environments:Models trained on one dataset may not
perform well in different environments due to changes in lighting, road materials,
or camera setups.
5.Limitations of Classical Models:Classical deep learning models may struggle
when feature representations are reduced to very low dimensions, as required in
hybrid quantum systems.
These challenges motivate the need for improved approaches that can effectively
capture complex visual patterns while maintaining robustness across different environ-
ments.
1.3 SCOPE OF THE PROJECT
This work focuses on the detection and classification of four types of road hazards:
cracks, potholes, open or displaced manholes, and garbage. A YOLO-based detector is
2
used to identify regions of interest during inference, while the main contribution lies in
the design of a hybrid quantum-classical classification model.
The proposed system follows a two-stage pipeline, where detection and classifica-
tion are handled separately. The classification stage combines a pre-trained ResNet-18
model for feature extraction, a multilayer perceptron for dimensionality reduction, and
a 4-qubit Variational Quantum Circuit for enhanced representation learning.
The quantum circuit is implemented using the PennyLane simulator, as execution
on physical quantum hardware is beyond the scope of this project. However, the circuit
design uses standard quantum gates such asRY rotations and CNOT operations, making
it compatible with current quantum computing platforms.
The dataset used in this work is constructed by merging multiple publicly available
sources and applying preprocessing techniques such as cropping, resizing, and normali-
sation. The system is evaluated using standard performance metrics including accuracy,
precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrix.
The scope of this project is limited to image-based classification and does not in-
clude real-time deployment or integration with live monitoring systems.

2.1 LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1.1 Object Detection for Road Hazards
Real-time object detection has improved significantly in recent years, particularly with
the development of single-stage detectors. The YOLO (You Only Look Once) family
has become widely used due to its ability to perform detection efficiently in a single
forward pass.
Wang et al. (2023) introduced YOLOv7, which achieved strong performance in
both speed and accuracy on benchmark datasets. The model incorporates architectural
improvements such as re-parameterised convolutions and improved training strategies,
making it suitable for real-time applications like road hazard detection.
Later, Wang et al. (2024) proposed YOLOv9 with Programmable Gradient Informa-
tion (PGI), which helps preserve useful information during training. This is particularly
useful for detecting small or partially visible objects such as cracks or damaged man-
holes.
Tran et al. (2023) compared multiple YOLO-based models for pavement inspection
tasks and found that single-stage detectors generally provide a good balance between
accuracy and inference speed. This supports the use of YOLO-based detection in prac-
tical systems.
Li et al. (2023) explored anchor-free detection approaches for road defects in chal-
lenging environments with poor lighting. Their work showed improved robustness and
reduced dependency on predefined anchor settings.
Other approaches such as transformer-based detectors and segmentation models
have also been explored. However, these methods often require higher computational
resources or dense annotations, which can limit their use in real-time systems.
4
2.1.2 Convolutional Neural Networks and Residual Architectures
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are widely used for extracting visual features
from images. Among them, residual networks (ResNets) remain a strong choice due to
their ability to train deeper models effectively.
Wightman et al. (2021) showed that well-optimised ResNet models continue to per-
form competitively on image classification tasks.
He et al. (2022) demonstrated that self-supervised pre-training methods improve the
generalisation ability of ResNet features, especially when labelled data is limited.
Nawaz et al. (2022) applied fine-tuned ResNet models to classify pavement cracks
and reported high classification accuracy.
Although Vision Transformers have also been explored, they generally require larger
datasets and higher computational resources. For moderate-sized datasets like road haz-
ard datasets, CNN-based models remain more practical.
2.1.3 Infrastructure Monitoring Systems
Recent advancements in sensing technologies and deep learning have enabled auto-
mated infrastructure monitoring systems.
Fan et al. (2022) proposed a real-time pothole detection system using stereo vision
and deep learning.
Bahnsen et al. (2022) introduced a multi-modal system combining camera and Li-
DAR data.
Xu et al. (2019) developed a transformer-based crack detection model that provides
accurate boundary segmentation.
These systems demonstrate the effectiveness of deep learning for road monitoring,
while also highlighting computational and scalability challenges.
2.1.4 Quantum Machine Learning
Quantum machine learning (QML) explores the use of quantum computing principles
to enhance machine learning models.
Cerezo et al. (2022) analysed variational quantum algorithms and discussed chal-
lenges such as the barren plateau problem.
5
Schuld and Petruccione (2022) provided a theoretical foundation for quantum fea-
ture maps and kernel methods.
Biamonte (2021) demonstrated that variational quantum circuits can approximate
general quantum operations.
Abbas et al. (2021) showed that quantum models can achieve higher representa-
tional capacity compared to classical models.
2.1.5 Hybrid Quantum-Classical Models
Hybrid quantum-classical models combine classical neural networks with quantum cir-
cuits.
Kashif and Al-Kuwari (2023) studied cost function design in hybrid models.
Altares-L´opez et al. (2021) applied hybrid models to image classification.
Huang et al. (2021) analysed the theoretical advantages of quantum-enhanced mod-
els.
Sagingalieva et al. (2023) applied hybrid models to real-world tasks with limited
data.
These studies indicate that hybrid approaches can be effective for classification
problems.
2.2 RESEARCH GAP
From the literature review, it is evident that deep learning-based approaches perform
well for road hazard detection. However, several limitations remain:
• Difficulty in generalising across different environments
• Class imbalance in real-world datasets
• Limited exploration of quantum-enhanced models in practical applications
• Lack of hybrid approaches applied specifically to road hazard classification
Additionally, most quantum machine learning studies are limited to benchmark
datasets and do not address real-world infrastructure problems.
6
2.3 PROBLEM STATEMENT
Road hazard detection and classification remain challenging due to variability in hazard
appearance, similarities between different classes, and limitations in available datasets.
Existing classical models may struggle to capture complex feature representations when
dimensionality is reduced.
Therefore, there is a need for a robust and efficient system that can accurately clas-
sify road hazards while handling real-world variability. This work proposes a hybrid
quantum-classical approach to address these challenges.
2.4 OBJECTIVES
The objectives of this project are:
1. To design a two-stage system separating detection and classification
2. To develop a hybrid quantum-classical model using ResNet-18, MLP, and a Vari-
ational Quantum Circuit
3. To construct and preprocess a balanced dataset from multiple sources
4. To train the model using parameter-shift-based gradient computation
5. To evaluate performance using standard classification metrics and compare with
a classical baseline
2.5 SUMMARY
This chapter reviewed existing work in road hazard detection, deep learning, and quan-
tum machine learning. Based on the identified research gaps, the problem statement
and objectives of the proposed system were defined.

CHAPTER 3
TECHNICAL SPECIFICATION
3.1 REQUIREMENTS
3.1.1 Functional Requirements
The functional requirements define the core operations that the proposed system must
perform:
• The system should accept input images containing road surfaces.
• The system should detect regions of interest (hazards) using a YOLO-based de-
tection model.
• The system should extract cropped regions corresponding to detected hazards.
• The system should preprocess images through resizing and normalisation.
• The system should classify hazards into one of four categories: crack, pothole,
open manhole, or garbage.
• The system should integrate classical deep learning with a quantum circuit for
classification.
• The system should output predicted class labels along with evaluation metrics.
3.1.2 Non-Functional Requirements
The non-functional requirements define the quality attributes of the system:
•Performance:The system should provide accurate classification results with
minimal latency.
•Scalability:The system should be capable of handling large datasets.
•Reliability:The system should produce consistent predictions under varying in-
put conditions.
8
•Maintainability:The modular design should allow easy updates and improve-
ments.
•Usability:The system should be simple to use for training and inference.
3.2 FEASIBILITY STUDY
3.2.1 Technical Feasibility
The system is technically feasible as it uses well-established frameworks such as Py-
Torch for deep learning and PennyLane for quantum simulation. The hybrid architec-
ture can be implemented using existing tools without requiring specialised hardware.
The use of a quantum simulator allows experimentation with quantum models while
avoiding limitations of current quantum hardware.
3.2.2 Economic Feasibility
The system is cost-effective as it relies on open-source tools and publicly available
datasets. No expensive hardware is required since the quantum component is simulated.
This makes the project suitable for academic research and scalable for future de-
ployment.
3.2.3 Operational Feasibility
The system can be integrated into existing road monitoring workflows. It can be used
by municipal authorities or infrastructure management systems to automate hazard de-
tection and classification.
The modular pipeline allows independent operation of detection and classification

CHAPTER 8
CONCLUSION AND FURTHER ENHANCEMENTS
8.1 CONCLUSION
This thesis presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of a quantum-enhanced
deep learning framework for road hazard safety analysis. The proposed system ad-
dresses the problem of automated road hazard detection and classification, which is im-
portant for urban infrastructure safety and smart city applications. A hybrid approach
combining classical deep learning and quantum computing techniques was developed
within a modular two-stage pipeline.
8.1.1 Summary of Contributions
The main contributions of this work are summarised below:
1. Modular Two-Stage Pipeline:A two-stage pipeline was designed, separat-
ing road hazard detection (YOLO-based) from classification (hybrid quantum-classical
model). This modular design allows independent optimisation of each stage and makes
the system easier to extend.
2. Hybrid Quantum-Classical Classifier:A hybrid model was developed consist-
ing of:
• A pre-trained ResNet-18 backbone producing a 512-dimensional feature vector
from 64×64 images.
• A two-layer MLP (512→64→4) for dimensionality reduction.
• A 4-qubit Variational Quantum Circuit withR Y encoding, 6 variational layers,
and CNOT-based entanglement.
• A classical linear layer with softmax output for final classification.
3. Class-Balanced Multi-Source Dataset:A dataset was constructed by merging
two publicly available datasets and applying class balancing through downsampling.
The final dataset includes 3,340 training, 740 validation, and 258 test images across
four hazard classes.
31
4. End-to-End Hybrid Training:The model was trained end-to-end using the
Adam optimiser. Classical gradients were computed using automatic differentiation,
while quantum gradients were obtained using the parameter-shift rule.
5. Comparative Evaluation:The hybrid model achieved a macro F1-score of
0.8439, outperforming the classical baseline by approximately 5%. The results demon-
strate that the quantum component contributes to improved classification performance.
8.1.2 Key Findings
The experimental results lead to the following observations:
1. The hybrid quantum-classical model is a viable approach for road hazard classi-
fication and performs competitively compared to a classical baseline.
2. Dataset quality and class balance have a strong influence on performance, often
more significant than model complexity.
3. The quantum component improves recall, which is particularly important for
safety-critical applications.
4. The hybrid model trains stably, and the parameter-shift rule provides reliable gra-
dient estimation.
5. The modular design simplifies system development and allows independent im-
provements to detection and classification stages.
8.1.3 Significance of the Work
This work demonstrates the practical applicability of hybrid quantum-classical models
in a real-world computer vision task. It provides a proof of concept for integrating
variational quantum circuits into infrastructure monitoring systems.
The proposed framework also contributes to smart city applications by enabling
automated road hazard detection, which can support maintenance planning and improve
road safety.
8.2 LIMITATIONS
Despite promising results, the current work has several limitations:
32
1.Quantum Simulation:The quantum circuit is executed on a simulator rather
than real hardware. Performance may differ when deployed on noisy quantum
devices.
2.Low Resolution Input:The use of 64×64 images may limit the model’s ability
to capture fine-grained details.
3.Limited Qubit Count:The model uses only 4 qubits, which restricts represen-
tational capacity.
4.Dataset Scope:The dataset includes only four hazard categories and limited
geographic diversity.
5.Barren Plateau Risk:Deeper quantum circuits may suffer from vanishing gra-
dients, making training difficult.

8.3.3 Scaling Qubit Count

8.3.7 Quantum Kernel Methods

8.3.8 Explainability
